AI Terms in Plain Terms

AI words, explained like a person would

Read this after AI Safety Basics. No math, no code, just what each word means, what it does, and what it does not mean. The terms build on each other, so read them in order the first time, then use this page as a reference.

How to read this page

The big picture

The big ideas first

Artificial intelligence (AI)

Software that does tasks which usually need human judgment, like understanding a sentence, recognizing a face in a photo, or deciding what to do next. The key word is tasks. AI is a tool that does specific jobs, not a mind.

Think of it like: a calculator for judgment calls. It does one kind of thinking fast, and it does not know it is doing it.

What it is not: not a person, not a brain, and not conscious. It has no goals of its own.

Machine learning

One way to build AI. Instead of a person writing every rule, the system studies many examples and finds the patterns itself. You show it thousands of emails labeled spam or not spam, and it learns what spam looks like.

Think of it like: learning to spot a ripe tomato by looking at a thousand tomatoes, instead of memorizing a written list of rules about color and firmness.

What it is not: not all AI is machine learning. Some AI still runs on rules written by hand.

Deep learning

Machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers. The layers let it handle messy inputs like photos, speech, and long text, where simple rules fail.

Think of it like: a chain of filters. Each layer picks out a little more detail, edges first, then shapes, then whole objects.

What it is not: not deeper thinking. Deep means many layers, not more understanding.

Neural network

A program made of layers of simple units that pass numbers to each other. Training adjusts how strongly each unit listens to the ones before it. The design is loosely inspired by brain cells, but the units are math, not cells.

Think of it like: a huge team where each person passes a number to the next row, and the team gets better at its job by adjusting who listens to whom.

What it is not: not a brain. It does not feel, remember, or know anything outside its numbers.

Generative AI

AI that makes new things: text, images, music, code. It learned patterns from examples, then uses those patterns to produce something new that fits them.

Think of it like: a musician who has heard ten thousand songs and can now write a new one in the same style. The song is new, the style is learned.

What it is not: not copying. It does not paste pieces of its training data together, and it does not create from nothing either. It produces new combinations of learned patterns.

The engine

Inside a language model

Large language model (LLM)

A very large neural network trained on huge amounts of text. Its core skill is predicting the next word, or the next piece of a word, based on the words so far. That one skill, done at huge scale, is what lets it write, summarize, translate, and answer questions.

Think of it like: autocomplete that has read a library. It guesses the most likely next words, and a good guess reads like a real answer.

What it is not: not a search engine and not a database. It does not look up facts. It generates text that fits the patterns it learned.

Model

The finished, trained program. Training builds the model. Using the model is what happens when you send a prompt.

Think of it like: a trained cook. The training is the years of practice. The model is the cook standing in your kitchen now.

What it is not: not the app around it. The chat window, the buttons, and the settings are the product. The model is the engine inside.

Training

The process of showing a model many examples so it adjusts its inner numbers to find patterns. Training takes a lot of computing power and happens before you ever use the model.

Think of it like: studying for an exam. You read the material, you get better at the subject, and then the exam is a different moment.

What it is not: not learning from you. Your chats do not retrain the model on the spot, unless a tool is specifically built to learn from them.

Training data

All the text, images, and other material used during training. What is in it shapes what the model can do, and what it gets wrong.

Think of it like: the books a student read. A student who only read cookbooks will not be good at car repair.

What it is not: not the model's memory. It does not store the training data and quote it back. It keeps patterns, not copies.

Parameters

The adjustable numbers inside a model. Training sets them. A model with more parameters can hold more patterns, which is why people talk about model size in billions.

Think of it like: the knobs on a sound board. More knobs give finer control, but the skill of the person setting them still matters.

What it is not: not knowledge in the human sense. A parameter is a number, not a fact the model believes.

Token

A small chunk of text that a model reads and writes. A token is often part of a word, not a whole word. Short words are usually one token. Long words split into several.

Think of it like: syllables. A model reads in syllable-sized pieces, not whole words.

What it is not: not the same as a word. Counting words and counting tokens give different numbers.

Context window

How much text a model can hold in mind at once during one conversation. It includes your messages and its answers so far. When the conversation gets longer than the window, the oldest parts drop out.

Think of it like: a desk. You can only spread so many pages on it. The pages that fall off the edge are gone from view.

What it is not: not memory. The model does not remember past chats. Each new chat starts with an empty desk.

Inference

The model doing its job after training: reading your prompt and generating an answer. Every reply you get is inference.

Think of it like: the exam after the studying. Training was preparation. Inference is performance.

What it is not: not learning. Answering you does not change the model.

Fine-tuning

Extra training on a smaller, focused set of examples, to make a general model better at one job, like answering customer questions in a company's tone.

Think of it like: a general cook taking a short course in baking. The general skill stays, the specialty gets sharper.

What it is not: not a new model from scratch. It starts from a trained model and adjusts it.

At the keyboard

Using it

Prompt

The instruction or question you type into an AI tool. It is the whole input: the task, the details, and any limits you set.

Think of it like: an order at a counter. The clearer the order, the closer the result is to what you wanted.

What it is not: not a command the model must obey. It guides the model, it does not control it.

Prompt engineering

The skill of writing prompts that get better answers: clear instructions, useful context, and examples when they help. That is what Parts 1 to 4 of this series teach.

Think of it like: learning to ask a good question. The answer improves because the question improved.

What it is not: not a trick or a secret code. It is clear communication, not hacking.

Hallucination

A confident answer that is invented. The model can make up facts, quotes, links, and citations that look real. It happens because the model predicts likely words, not checked facts.

Think of it like: a fluent storyteller who fills a gap in memory with a smooth invention instead of saying I do not know.

What it is not: not a lie. A lie needs intent. A hallucination is a prediction error that sounds certain.

Bias

An unfair pattern in what the model produces. It usually comes from the training data: if the examples it learned from show a stereotype, the model can repeat it.

Think of it like: a student who only ever saw one kind of example, and assumes that is how the world always looks.

What it is not: not a political opinion the model holds. It is a pattern in the data and in how the model was built.

Chatbot

A program you talk to in a conversation, usually in a chat window. Many chatbots today use a language model as their engine.

Think of it like: a shop assistant behind a counter. The counter is the chat window. The assistant is the model.

What it is not: not the same as an LLM. The chatbot is the app. The LLM is the engine inside it.

Multimodal

A model that works with more than one kind of input, for example text and images together. It can read a photo and answer a question about it.

Think of it like: a person who can both read and look. One sense alone gives half the story.

What it is not: not several models glued together, though early versions were. A multimodal model is trained to handle the kinds together.

AGI

Artificial general intelligence: the idea of an AI as capable as a person across all kinds of tasks, not just one. It is a goal and a debate, not a product. No AI today is AGI.

Think of it like: the difference between a champion chess player and a person who can also cook, drive, and learn a new game from scratch.

What it is not: not here, and not close by any measure everyone agrees on. Claims about when it arrives are guesses, not facts.

Do not mix these up

Terms people mix up

Six pairs that sound alike and mean different things. If you can tell each pair apart, you already know more than most people talking about AI.

AI vs machine learningMachine learning is one way to build AI. All machine learning is AI, but not all AI is machine learning.
Training vs inferenceTraining is the model learning from examples. Inference is the model answering you. One happens before, the other happens now.
LLM vs chatbotThe LLM is the engine. The chatbot is the app you talk to, built around that engine.
Token vs wordA token is a piece of text, often part of a word. Words and tokens are counted differently.
Model vs training dataTraining data is what the model learned from. The model is what training produced. It keeps patterns, not copies.
Bias vs hallucinationBias is a slant in real patterns the model learned. Hallucination is an invented answer that was never in the patterns at all.

Common misunderstandings

  • “AI thinks like a person”: it predicts patterns. It does not understand, believe, or intend. Fix: say what it does, not what it seems to do.
  • “Bigger always means smarter”: more parameters help, but data quality, design, and the task matter too. Fix: ask what the model was trained for, not just how big it is.
  • “The model remembers facts like a database”: it keeps patterns from training, not copies of documents. Fix: expect it to be wrong about specific facts, and check them.
  • “A token is a word”: tokens are pieces of text, often parts of words. Fix: count tokens and words separately.
  • “AGI is already here”: no AI today is generally capable like a person. Fix: treat AGI claims as guesses, and ask what the claim is based on.
