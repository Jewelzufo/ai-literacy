AI Safety Basics

Stay safe with AI: protect yourself before you prompt

Read this page first, before Parts 1 to 4. Good prompting starts with safe prompting: what never to paste, how to check a prompt in 30 seconds, and how to use AI fairly. Nothing here needs technical skill, only habits you can start today.

Start here

What AI safety means

An AI chat feels like a private conversation. It is not. What you type can be stored on company servers, read by reviewers, and used to train future models. That does not make AI dangerous. It makes the chat box a place to be careful, like a public post, not a diary.

The one rule: if you would not want it on a billboard, do not paste it into an AI chat.

Three facts worth knowing

  • Chats are stored. Most AI tools keep your conversations on their servers, even the free ones.
  • Deleting a chat does not always delete the data right away. Companies may keep copies for a time, for safety and legal reasons.
  • Free tools are often paid for with data. That is a fair trade only when you know it. Check the settings, and choose what you share.

The red list

Never paste these into an AI chat

If a prompt needs one of these to work, replace it with a blank, like [Name] or [Account number], and fill it in yourself after the AI answers.

  • Passwords and PINs. Never, for any account, for any reason.
  • Your full name together with your address, phone, or email. One piece alone is often fine. Together, they identify you.
  • ID numbers. Social security, passport, driver's license, or any government number.
  • Bank and card numbers. Account numbers, full card numbers, and security codes.
  • Health records. Lab results, prescriptions, diagnoses, and anything from a medical portal, with your name on it.
  • Children's details. Names, ages, schools, photos, and anything about their health.
  • Work secrets. Client names, prices, internal documents, and anything your job calls confidential.
  • Photos of documents. IDs, tickets, bills, and screens that show personal data in the background.

Before you send

The 30-second check

Read your prompt once before you send it. Ask four questions. If the answer to any of them is yes, take that part out first.

  1. Would I mind if this were public? If yes, it does not belong in the chat.
  2. Does it identify me, or someone else? Names, addresses, and numbers, alone or combined.
  3. Could someone use it to steal from me, or pretend to be me? Passwords, codes, account details, and answers to security questions.
  4. Is it someone else's private information? A friend, a child, a client, or a coworker did not agree to share it.

When in doubt, leave it out. A prompt with a blank still works. The AI will ask for the detail, or you can add it yourself after.

Your account

Lock down your account

  • Use a password you use nowhere else, and turn on two-factor authentication, the code from your phone at sign in, when the tool offers it.
  • Look for data controls in settings. Many tools have a switch for "use my chats to train models". Turn it off if you prefer your chats stay out of training.
  • Delete old chats you no longer need, especially ones that hold personal details.
  • Do not connect the AI to your email, bank, or work accounts unless you understand what it can read and what it can do there.
  • On a shared computer or phone, sign out when you are done. Your chats are your account, and they stay open until you close them.

Check the output

Do not trust every answer

AI can sound confident and be wrong. It invents facts, quotes, links, and citations that look real. That is not a bug you can fix with a better prompt. It is how these tools work, so checking is part of using them.

  • Check anything that affects money, health, or legal decisions with a real source or a real person before you act on it.
  • Open the link yourself. If the AI gives you a source, click it and read it. A fake link can look perfect.
  • Be extra careful with urgency, prizes, and payments. AI tools can also write convincing scam messages, so a well written message is not proof it is honest.
  • Photos, voices, and videos can be faked. A familiar face or voice on a call is not proof on its own. Call back on a number you already know.

Brief overview

AI ethics, in plain terms

Safety protects you. Ethics is about how your use of AI affects other people. Five ideas cover most of it.

FairnessAI learns from the internet, so it can repeat stereotypes and biases. Read outputs about people with care, and question a result that treats a whole group unfairly.
HonestyDo not use AI to deceive. Fake reviews, fake credentials, and fake messages are still lying, even when a machine writes them.
CreditSay when AI helped. Do not present AI work as entirely your own, at school, at work, or in anything you publish.
ConsentDo not feed other people's photos, voices, writing, or private messages into an AI without their permission.
ImpactOne person's misuse is small. The same misuse by a million people is not. Think about who is affected if everyone did what you are about to do.
The ethics ruleIf you would not do it with a human assistant watching, do not do it with an AI.

Before you trust it

Safety checklist, for every prompt

  • Did I remove names, addresses, numbers, and passwords before sending?
  • Did I run the 30-second check?
  • Did I check the settings: training off if I want it off, old chats deleted?
  • Did I verify any fact that affects money, health, or legal choices?
  • Did I say when AI helped, and did I use anyone else's material only with permission?
  • Am I reusing safe prompts from a saved note, instead of retyping personal details each time?

Remember: a safe prompt is not a weaker prompt. Blanks and general details still get good answers, and they keep your life out of the training data.

Common failure modes

  • “It is just a chat”: treating the chat box as private. Fix: assume it is stored.
  • “I will delete it later”: personal details already sent cannot be unsent. Fix: clean the prompt before sending, not after.
  • “The AI said it, so it is true”: confident tone is not evidence. Fix: verify from a second source.
  • “It is only one photo”: one photo can carry names, locations, and documents in the background. Fix: check the image before uploading.
  • “Everyone does it”: using AI to fake reviews or homework because others do. Fix: the ethics rule above.

Reference

Glossary: safety and ethics terms

Short, plain definitions for the new terms used on this page. The Part 2 glossary covers Context, Role, Goal, and Constraints.

Personal data
Any information that identifies you, such as your name, address, phone, email, or photo.
Sensitive data
Personal data that can cause real harm if exposed, such as passwords, ID numbers, bank details, or health records.
Training data
The text, images, and other material an AI learns from. Some tools add your chats to it unless you opt out.
Hallucination
A confident AI answer that is invented, including fake facts, quotes, or links.
Phishing
A fake message designed to trick you into sharing passwords, codes, or money.
Deepfake
A fake photo, voice, or video made to look like a real person.
Bias
An unfair pattern in AI output, often learned from biased material in training data.
Consent
Permission from a person before you use their photo, voice, writing, or private information.
Plagiarism
Presenting someone else's work, or AI output, as your own without saying so.
Two-factor authentication
A second check at sign in, usually a code from your phone, in addition to your password.
Data retention
How long a company keeps your chats and data after you send them.
Opt out
A setting that stops a company from using your chats to train its models.
