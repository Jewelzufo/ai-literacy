Prompt Template, Part 6

Ask for the steps, not just the answer

Read after Part 5. When an answer needs thinking, ask the AI to show its thinking. Steps you can read are steps you can check, and a wrong step is easy to spot and fix.

The idea

What chain-of-thought is

Chain-of-thought means asking the AI to work through a problem step by step, and to show each step, before it gives the final answer. The steps are the chain. The answer is the last link.

The one rule: steps make reasoning checkable. A bare answer can only be believed or doubted. A stepped answer can be checked, one step at a time.

How to ask for it

  • "Work it out step by step." The simplest version, and it works for most math and planning jobs.
  • "Show each step, then give the final answer." Use this when you want the steps and the answer kept separate, so the answer is easy to find.
  • "Explain your reasoning as you go." Use this when the steps are judgments, not numbers, like comparing two offers.

Put the request in the Goal of your template, and say what the steps should cover: the numbers, the comparison, or the plan, in order.

The 2026 caveat

Newer models already reason inside

Modern reasoning models think step by step on their own, even when you do not ask. They work through the problem before they answer. Asking for the steps still matters, for three reasons.

  • You need to see the steps. If the answer affects money or a decision, you want to read how it got there, not just trust it.
  • Extended thinking may be off or unavailable. Some tools let you turn deep thinking off, and some smaller models do not do it at all. Asking for steps fills that gap.
  • Steps catch slips. A wrong number in step two is easy to spot when the steps are written out, and hard to spot in a bare answer.

Do not ask for steps on every prompt. Ask when the answer needs checking, and skip it when a simple fact is all you need.

When to use it

Use steps for thinking jobs, not for facts

  • Use it for math. Splitting bills, working out budgets, checking totals, and word problems.
  • Use it for planning. Schedules, trips, and projects, where each step depends on the one before it.
  • Use it for comparing options. Two plans, two prices, or two offers, where the winner depends on several numbers.
  • Use it to check work. When you already have an answer and want to know if it is right, ask the AI to work it out step by step and compare.
  • Skip it for simple facts. "What is the capital of France?" needs no chain.
  • Skip it for creative writing. Steps make a story or a poem stiff, and there is nothing to check.
Answer only, nothing to check“How much does each of us owe for dinner?” The AI gives a number. If the number is wrong, you have no way to find where it went wrong.
Steps first, answer last“Work it out step by step: add the bill, add the tip, split it four ways, and show each step. Then give the amount each person owes.” Every step can be checked.
Steps that restate the answer“Step 1: Plan A is cheaper. Step 2: Plan B is more expensive. Step 3: Pick Plan A.” These are not steps, they are the answer said three times.
Steps that do the work“Step 1: monthly cost of each plan. Step 2: data included. Step 3: fees over 12 months. Step 4: total cost of each. Step 5: which is cheaper, and by how much.”

Visual 1 of 5 • Math

Split a dinner bill, step by step

The job: Four friends split a dinner bill of $86.40, with a 15 percent tip. You want the amount each person owes, and you want to check the math.

The bill, worked in the open

  1. Step 1: the tip amount$86.40 times 15 percent = $12.96
  2. Step 2: the total with tip$86.40 plus $12.96 = $99.36
  3. Step 3: split four ways$99.36 divided by 4 = $24.84 each

What to notice: Round only at the end, and give the amount each person owes last. If any step shows a different number, that step is where the error is.

Visual 2 of 5 • Planning

Plan a day trip, step by step

The job: You want a day trip plan: leave at 9 am, visit a beach and a market, be home by 6 pm. A bare list of places does not show whether the day fits.

The day, fitted to the hours

  1. Step 1: travel to the beachLeave home at 9 am. The drive is one hour each way.
  2. Step 2: time at the beachMorning at the beach, with time to spare.
  3. Step 3: lunchA break in the middle of the day, not squeezed between drives.
  4. Step 4: time at the marketAfternoon at the market.
  5. Step 5: travel homeArriving by 6 pm, with at least 30 minutes of buffer.

What to notice: A bare list of places does not show whether the day fits. Add up the step times yourself: if the plan runs past 6 pm, the steps show which part to shorten.

Visual 3 of 5 • Comparing

Compare two phone plans over 12 months

The job: Plan A costs $30 a month with 5 GB of data. Plan B costs $45 a month with unlimited data and a $50 sign up fee. Which is cheaper over a year depends on more than the monthly price.

The plans, costed step by step

  1. Step 1: monthly cost of each planPlan A: $30. Plan B: $45.
  2. Step 2: extra data cost for Plan AYou use 8 GB, Plan A includes 5 GB, so 3 GB extra at $10 each = $30 more. Plan A is really $60 a month.
  3. Step 3: sign up fee for Plan B$50, once.
  4. Step 4: 12 month total of each planPlan A: $60 times 12 = $720. Plan B: $45 times 12 plus $50 = $590.
  5. Step 5: which is cheaper, and by how muchPlan B, cheaper by $130.

What to notice: The monthly price alone misleads: Plan A looks cheaper at $30 against $45, but the extra data flips it. The steps show why.

Visual 4 of 5 • Checking

Check a word problem, step by step

The job: A recipe for 4 people uses 2 cups of flour. You need it for 6 people. Your first guess is 3 cups, but you want to check the work before you bake.

The recipe, checked before you bake

  1. Step 1: flour per person2 cups for 4 people = half a cup per person.
  2. Step 2: flour for 6 peopleHalf a cup times 6 = 3 cups.
  3. Step 3: was the guess right?The guess was 3 cups. The steps say it was right.

What to notice: The steps matter more than the answer here: if the guess had been wrong, the steps would show where.

Visual 5 of 5 • Your turn

Use steps on your own reasoning job

The job: Pick one task you do with an LLM at least once a week where the answer needs checking: a budget, a schedule, a comparison, or a calculation. Build the chain-of-thought version of its prompt, then save it.

Your chain-of-thought prompt, as a routine

  1. Pick the jobA budget, a schedule, a comparison, or a calculation, one you do at least once a week.
  2. Name the stepsWhat must be worked out, in order, before the answer?
  3. Ask for the math at each stepShow the work, round only at the end, give the final answer last.
  4. Check a step, not just the answerIf a number looks wrong, the step that made it is where the error is.
  5. Save itKeep the prompt for the next time the same job comes up.

Before you trust it

Chain-of-thought checklist, for every prompt

  • Did I ask for the steps, not just the answer?
  • Did I say what the steps should cover, in order?
  • Did I ask for the final answer last, after the steps?
  • Did I give the AI the numbers or facts it must work from?
  • Did I check each step myself, instead of only reading the answer?
  • Did I skip the steps when the job was a simple fact or creative writing?

Remember: steps are for checking, not for show. If you never read the steps, they are decoration.

Common failure modes

  • “Steps that restate the answer”: each step repeats the conclusion instead of doing work. Fix: name what each step must compute or decide.
  • “Steps that skip the hard part”: the chain jumps from the question to the answer. Fix: ask for the middle steps by name.
  • “Trusting the steps without checking”: a wrong step early on makes every later step wrong. Fix: check each step, starting with the first.
  • “Steps on a fact question”: asking for a chain when a one line fact would do. Fix: save steps for thinking jobs.
  • “Steps with no numbers”: the AI works from guessed figures instead of yours. Fix: put your numbers in the prompt.

Reference

Glossary: chain-of-thought terms

Short, plain definitions for the new terms used on this page. The Part 2 glossary covers Context, Role, Goal, and Constraints.

Chain-of-thought
Asking the AI to show its work step by step before the final answer.
Reasoning
Working through a problem in order, where each step builds on the one before it.
Step
One piece of the work, such as one calculation or one comparison, shown on its own.
Working
The steps and numbers the AI shows, so you can follow how it reached the answer.
Checkable
Written so a person can verify each part, instead of having to trust the whole answer at once.
Extended thinking
A setting on some AI tools that lets the model spend more time thinking before it answers.
Reasoning model
An AI model built to work through problems step by step on its own, before answering.
Hallucination
A confident AI answer that is invented, including fake facts, quotes, or links.
Arithmetic
Plain number work: adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing.
Assumption
A fact the AI takes for granted without checking, such as a price or a number you never gave.
Verification
Checking an answer against a real source, a second calculation, or your own math.
Final answer
The conclusion given after the steps, kept separate so it is easy to find.
