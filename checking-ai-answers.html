Checking AI Answers

Do not act on the first answer: check it first

AI answers sound confident, even when they are wrong. That is not a flaw you can fix with a better prompt. It is how these tools work. This page teaches you a short routine for checking an answer before you act on it, and how to tell which answers always need a real source or a real person.

Start here

Why AI sounds sure when it is wrong

A language model writes the words most likely to come next. It does not look up facts, and it does not know when it is guessing. So a wrong answer can read exactly like a right one: smooth, detailed, and confident.

The longer and more detailed the answer, the more tempting it is to trust it. Length is not evidence. Detail is not evidence. Confidence is not evidence. Only a check is evidence.

The routine

The five-step check

  1. Pause. Ask: does this answer matter? If it affects money, health, safety, or a legal choice, it needs a check.
  2. Ask for the source. Ask the AI where the fact came from, and what page or document it is quoting.
  3. Open the source yourself. Click the link and read the page. A fake link can look perfect, and a real link can say something different from what the AI claimed.
  4. Check a second source. One source can be wrong. A second, separate source that agrees is much stronger.
  5. Ask a human for high stakes. For money, health, or legal decisions, a real person who knows the subject beats any AI answer.

Match the check to the stakes. A quick check takes a minute. A deep check takes longer. A trivia question needs less than a loan decision, but a loan decision always needs the full routine.

High stakes

What always needs a human check

  • Money. Fees, prices, contracts, taxes, investments, and anything you pay for or sign.
  • Health. Symptoms, medicines, doses, test results, and treatment choices. Ask a doctor, not a chatbot.
  • Legal. Rights, deadlines, forms, fines, and anything a court or government office decides.
  • Safety. Instructions for tools, chemicals, repairs, or anything that could hurt you if wrong.
  • Other people's facts. Claims about a real person, a company, or an event, before you repeat them or publish them.

For these, the AI is a starting point, never the final word.

Ask better

Make the AI show its work

You can ask the AI to make checking easier. Add one of these to your prompt:

  • "Name your source, and quote the exact line."
  • "If you are not sure, say so. Do not guess."
  • "Show the steps, so I can check each one."
  • "Give me the answer only from this source:" then paste the source.

Even then, check. An AI asked for a source can still invent one, so the source only counts once you have opened it yourself.

Two sources

Why two AI chats agreeing is not two sources

Two AI tools can give the same wrong answer, because they learned from similar material and make similar guesses. Agreement between AIs is not independent evidence.

A second source means a separate place that did its own checking: an official page, a news outlet, a book, or a person who knows the subject. One AI plus one real source beats two AIs.

Check a loan fee before you pay it

Verify the fee before you pay it

  1. Open the bank fee page yourselfDo not trust the link without opening it.
  2. Search for the fee scheduleFind the late fee line and read the exact words.
  3. Check a second sourceThe loan agreement, or a call to the bank.
  4. Then payOnly after the bank itself confirms the $35 fee.

What to notice: Two sources beat one confident answer. The cost of skipping the check is paying a fee you do not owe, or missing a real one.

Check the symptom, then the doctor decides

  1. Check a trusted medical siteLook for the warning signs list for this symptom.
  2. Compare what you feelDo any of the warning signs match?
  3. Call your doctor if any of them matchA human check, not a second AI chat.
The AI can explain general information, but it cannot diagnose you.

What to notice: “Usually harmless” is the answer that most needs a human check, because it is the one you are tempted to believe without checking.

Visual 3 of 5 • School

Check a fact for schoolwork

The job: The AI gives you a date and a quote for an essay, and the quote sounds perfect. Build your check plan before you hand the essay in.

Verify the date and the quote

  1. Search the law by nameFind the real date the law passed.
  2. Search the quote in quotation marksIf no real source has the quote, the AI made it up.
  3. Check your textbook and one trusted siteTwo real sources, both opened by you.
  4. Then write it inCite the real source, not the AI, and say when the AI helped.

What to notice: Quotes are the AI's favorite invention, because they sound exact and are easy to fake.

Visual 4 of 5 • Safety

Check instructions that could go wrong

The job: The AI gives you steps to fix a leaking pipe under your sink, including which valve to turn and what tool to use. Build your check plan before you touch anything.

Verify the repair steps

  1. Check a second guide with photosText alone is not enough for a repair.
  2. Watch a repair video from a trusted sourceSee the valve and the trap before you touch them.
  3. Know your stop point before you startThe moment something looks different from the guide, stop.
If water starts spraying, shut off the main water and call a plumber. Do not force a rusted valve.

What to notice: Safety instructions are high stakes. A photo or video from a trusted source beats text alone.

Build your own check routine

The job: Pick one kind of AI answer you act on often, such as facts for work, health questions, or instructions. Build the check routine you will use every time, then save it.

Your check routine, as a routine

  1. Pick one kind of AI answerFacts for work, health questions, instructions: the kind you act on most often.
  2. Name the stakesWhat goes wrong if this answer is wrong? Money, health, safety, marks.
  3. List the checksTwo or three steps, in order, with the sources you will open.
  4. Use it every timeSave the routine and run it before you act, not after.

Before you act

Checking answers checklist

  • Did I ask whether this answer matters enough to check?
  • Did I ask the AI for its source, and open that source myself?
  • Did I check a second, separate source?
  • For money, health, legal, or safety, did I ask a real person?
  • Did I check the steps, not just the final answer?
  • Am I acting on a checked fact, or on a confident tone?

Remember: checking is not distrust. It is how you use a tool that guesses. A checked answer is worth more than a fast one.

Common failure modes

  • "The answer was long and detailed": length is not evidence. Fix: check the facts, not the format.
  • "It gave a link, so it must be true": links can be fake or misread. Fix: open the link and read the page.
  • "Two AI chats agreed": they can share the same mistake. Fix: find a real, separate source.
  • "I checked once, that is enough": one source can be wrong. Fix: check a second source for anything that matters.
  • "Checking takes too long": an unchecked mistake costs more. Fix: match the check to the stakes, but never skip it for high stakes.

Reference

Glossary: checking answers terms

Short, plain definitions for the new terms used on this page.

Hallucination
A confident AI answer that is invented, including fake facts, quotes, or links.
Source
Where a claim comes from, such as a page, a document, a person, or a dataset.
Citation
A note that points to the source of a fact, so you can find it and check it yourself.
Quote
The exact words from a source, copied word for word.
Verification
Checking a claim against a trusted source before you believe it or act on it.
Fact-check
The act of checking a claim against reliable sources to see if it is true.
Primary source
The original place a fact comes from, such as the law itself, the official page, or the person who said it.
Secondary source
A place that reports or explains a fact from a primary source, such as a news article about a law.
Confidence
How sure an answer sounds. In AI, confidence is a style, not a measure of truth.
Bias
An unfair pattern in AI output, often learned from biased material in training data.
Inference
The model doing its job after training: reading your prompt and generating an answer.
Grounding
Asking the AI to answer only from a source you give it, and to say when the answer is not there.
