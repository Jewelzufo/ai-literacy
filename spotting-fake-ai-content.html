Spotting Fake AI Content

Real or fake? How to spot AI-made content

AI can now make photos, voices, videos, and messages that look and sound real. Most of it is harmless fun. Some of it is built to trick you. This page teaches you how to pause, look closely, and check before you believe it or share it. Read it after AI Safety Basics.

Start here

What fake AI content is

AI tools can create new photos, voices, videos, and text in seconds. That new content is called synthetic media. It can be used for movies, jokes, and art. It can also be used to fake a person, fake an event, or fake a message from someone you trust.

The hard part is that the fakes keep getting better. The goal is not to spot every fake perfectly. The goal is to slow down and check the things that matter before you believe them, share them, or send money because of them.

Three kinds you will meet

  • Fake photos and videos. A person who does not exist, an event that never happened, or a famous person saying words they never said.
  • Fake voices. A phone call that sounds like a family member, a boss, or a bank worker, made by cloning a real voice from a short recording.
  • Fake messages. Scam texts and emails written by AI, with perfect spelling and a convincing story, asking you to pay, click, or share a code.

Look closely

Fake photos: what to look for

No single sign proves a photo is fake, and a real photo can have a strange detail. Look for several signs together, and check the source either way.

  • Hands and fingers. Too many fingers, fingers that bend the wrong way, or hands that melt into objects.
  • Text in the image. Signs, shirts, and labels with misspelled words, or letters that turn into shapes.
  • Skin and hair. Skin that looks too smooth, like plastic, or hair that blends into the background.
  • Eyes and ears. Eyes that look in different directions, earrings that do not match, or ears with odd shapes.
  • Background. Objects that blend together, straight lines that bend, or people in the background with blurred or missing faces.

Remember: a photo with no strange signs can still be fake. The signs help you pause. The source check is what settles it.

Listen closely

Fake video and voice: what to look for

  • Lip sync. The mouth moves a little out of time with the words, or the face looks stiff while the voice is full of feeling.
  • Blinking and expression. Too little blinking, or a face that never changes expression during an emotional story.
  • Urgent voice calls. The caller is urgent, asks for money or a code, and does not want you to hang up and call back. A real bank or family member will let you call back on a number you already know.
  • The call-back rule. If a call asks for money, a password, or a code, hang up and call the person or company yourself on a number you already have. Do not use a number the caller gives you.

Remember: voice cloning needs only a short recording of a real voice, sometimes taken from social media. A familiar voice on the phone is not proof of who is calling.

Read closely

Fake messages and scams: what to look for

  • Urgency. "Act now", "Your account closes today", "Pay in the next hour". Urgency is the scammer's favorite tool.
  • Prizes and payments. You won something you never entered, or you owe money for something you never bought.
  • A code or a link. A message asks for a code sent to your phone, or asks you to click a link to fix a problem.
  • Impersonation. The message claims to be your bank, a delivery company, a boss, or a family member, and asks for something unusual.

Remember: AI can now write perfect messages, so spelling mistakes no longer prove a message is fake, and perfect spelling no longer proves it is real. Check the sender and the request, not the grammar.

Before you share

The check-before-you-share routine

Use this routine for any photo, video, or claim that surprises you, angers you, or asks you to act fast. It takes about a minute.

  1. Pause. Do not share, reply, or pay while you feel rushed or upset.
  2. Check the source. Who posted it? Is it a news outlet you know, or an account you have never seen?
  3. Search the claim. Type the main words into a search engine. If only one strange site reports it, be careful.
  4. Check the image. Use a reverse image search to see if the photo appeared before, in a different place or year.
  5. Look for a second source. A real event is reported by more than one trusted place.
  6. Ask someone. If it asks for money or a code, ask a family member or call the company directly.

If you cannot verify it, do not share it. Not sharing is always a safe choice.

Check a viral photo before sharing it

The job: A photo shows a flooded street in your town. The account that posted it is new, has few followers, and the caption says the bridge collapsed this morning. Decide whether it is safe to share.

Check the photo before you share it

  1. Reverse image search the photoHas the same photo appeared before, from another place or year?
  2. Search local news for the floodDoes a local source report a flood in your town today?
  3. Check the accountNew account, few followers, urgent caption: three warning signs at once.
  4. Then decideShare only if the photo, the place, and the date all check out.

What to notice: If the reverse image search shows the same photo from another place or year, the post is fake, even if the flood itself is real somewhere else.

A voice call that sounds like family

The job: You get a call that sounds like your grandson. He says he is in trouble and needs money sent right now, and he begs you not to tell anyone. Build your response plan before you send anything.

The call-back routine

  1. Hang upDo not stay on the call. Do not send money, and do not use an account number the caller gives you.
  2. Call your grandson on his saved numberThe number you already have, not one the caller gives you.
  3. Call his parentsA second voice you trust, on a number you already have.
  4. Ask a question only he would knowSomething a stranger could not guess.
  5. No money moves until you have spoken to the real personThe call-back rule ends this scam every time.
“Do not tell anyone” is the scammer's shield. A real family member in trouble wants you to call for help.

What to notice: Urgency plus secrecy is the pattern. The call itself is the trap, not the story in it.

Visual 3 of 5 • Message

A prize message that asks for a code

The job: A text says you won a $500 gift card and asks you to reply with the code that was just sent to your phone. Build your check plan.

Prize message, or trap?

What the message wantsYou won a $500 gift card. Reply with the code that was just sent to your phone. It expires in 30 minutes.
What a real prize doesA real prize never asks for a code sent to your phone, and never rushes you with a 30 minute deadline.

What to do with it

  1. Do not replyDo not share the code, and do not click a link in the message.
  2. Check the senderA number you do not know, for a prize you never entered to win.
  3. Delete the messageThe code is usually a password reset code for one of your accounts. Sharing it hands the scammer the keys.

What to notice: Three warning signs at once: a prize you never entered, a code sent to your phone, and a deadline that rushes you.

Visual 4 of 5 • News

A news claim with a dramatic photo

The job: A post claims a famous person was arrested, with a photo of them in handcuffs outside a courthouse. It has thousands of shares. Build your check plan before you share it.

Check the claim before you share it

  1. Search the name with a trusted news outletDoes a newsroom you trust report the arrest?
  2. Reverse image search the photoIs the photo from this event, or from another time and place?
  3. Look for a second sourceOne trusted source is a start. Two is a check.
  4. Then decideDo not share until a trusted source confirms it. Share counts are not evidence.

What to notice: Thousands of shares mean thousands of people skipped the check, not that the claim is true.

Visual 5 of 5 • Your turn

Build your own check routine

The job: Pick one kind of content you see often, such as photos, videos, voice calls, or prize messages. Build the check routine you will use every time you meet it, then save it.

Your check routine, as a routine

  1. Pick one kind of contentPhotos, videos, voice calls, or prize messages: the kind you see most often.
  2. List its warning signsNew account, urgent caption, a code, a deadline, a request for secrecy.
  3. Write the checkTwo or three steps, in order, that you can do every time.
  4. Use it every timeSave the routine where you will find it, and run it before you share or send anything.

Before you share

Spotting fakes checklist

  • Did I pause before sharing, replying, or paying?
  • Did I check who posted it, and whether I know the source?
  • Did I search the claim and look for a second source?
  • Did I check the image with a reverse image search?
  • If it asked for money or a code, did I call back on a number I already have?
  • Am I sharing something I verified, or something that only surprised me?

Remember: a fake that fools you is not your failure. The people who make fakes are skilled, and the tools are powerful. The routine is what protects you, not your eyes alone.

Common failure modes

  • "It looks real, so it is real": fakes are built to look real. Fix: check the source, not the look.
  • "It came from a friend": friends share fakes too. Fix: check it yourself before passing it on.
  • "I will share it and check later": shares travel fast, corrections do not. Fix: check first, share second.
  • "One sign proves it is fake": one odd detail can appear in a real photo. Fix: look for several signs, then check the source.
  • "The detector said it is real": AI detectors guess, and they are often wrong. Fix: use the routine, not a detector score.

Reference

Glossary: fake content terms

Short, plain definitions for the new terms used on this page.

Deepfake
A fake photo, voice, or video made to look like a real person.
Voice clone
A fake voice built from a short recording of a real person's voice.
Synthetic media
Photos, video, audio, or text made by AI instead of recorded in real life.
Misinformation
False information shared by people who may not know it is false.
Disinformation
False information spread on purpose, to mislead people.
Phishing
A fake message designed to trick you into sharing passwords, codes, or money.
Reverse image search
Searching the web with a photo instead of words, to see where that photo appeared before.
Hallucination
A confident AI answer that is invented, including fake facts, quotes, or links.
Source
Where a claim or a piece of content comes from, such as a news outlet, a person, or a website.
Verification
Checking a claim or a piece of content against a trusted source before you believe it or share it.
Scam
A trick designed to steal money, passwords, or personal information.
Impersonation
Pretending to be someone else, such as a bank, a company, or a family member.
